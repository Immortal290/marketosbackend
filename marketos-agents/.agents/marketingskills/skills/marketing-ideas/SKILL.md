# Marketing Ideas

## What makes a marketing idea actually good

An idea is good if it is: (a) true to the product, (b) new in the category, (c) executable by the team, and (d) designed to spread. Generic brainstorm outputs fail on (b) and (d). Every idea below must pass the "why would someone forward this?" test.

## Idea generation frameworks

### The Enemy Framework
Name the villain your product defeats. Campaigns built around a shared enemy generate more word-of-mouth than benefit-first campaigns.
Example: "We killed the marketing spreadsheet" — not "we built a better spreadsheet."

### The Transparency Gambit
Share numbers, process, or failures your competitors hide. Radical openness in B2B creates outsized attention.
Examples: Sharing real churn rates, posting failed campaign results with lessons, publishing actual revenue growth.

### The Before/After Flip
Document the customer's before state in painful, specific detail. Then show the after. Let the contrast do the selling.
Best format: "We used to [painful thing]. Now [specific quantified better thing]. Here's what changed."

### The Benchmark Challenge
Create a piece of content that lets readers measure themselves against a standard.
Example: "The 20-point email deliverability audit" — readers discover their own score, share results, seek the fix.

### The Counter-Narrative
Take the conventional wisdom in your space and argue the opposite with evidence.
Example: "Why we stopped sending weekly newsletters (and what happened to our revenue)"

### The Serialized Case Study
One customer's story, told in 5 weekly installments. Each ends on a cliffhanger. Retention of series readers is 4–8x higher than one-shot case studies.

## Campaign idea quality filters

Before approving any idea, pass it through:
1. **"So what?" test** — If a reader says "so what?" after hearing it, the idea isn't sharp enough. Sharpen until the response is "wait, really?"
2. **"Only us" test** — Could a competitor run this campaign with their name swapped in? If yes, kill it or make it specific to your product's actual differentiation.
3. **Friction audit** — How many clicks or decisions are required before the reader gets value? Every extra step loses 30–50% of audience.
4. **Format fit** — Is this idea matched to the right distribution channel? A complex framework needs long-form. A single provocative claim needs a one-liner.

## Anti-patterns that waste creative budget

- Trending topic hijacking without genuine product relevance — audiences spot the opportunism and it backfires.
- "We're excited to announce" posts — no one is excited on your behalf. Show the result, not the announcement.
- Awards campaigns ("we won [award]") — these perform well internally and near-zero externally.
- Quoting your own CEO without an actual contrarian or surprising point of view.
